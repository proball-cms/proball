If there’s one question parents ask us more than almost any other, it’s this. _“How often should my child train?”_ 

Some parents worry they’re not doing enough. Others worry they’re doing too much. Then there are parents who look at families training four or five times a week and wonder if they’re falling behind.

The honest answer is… it depends. Not on how talented your child is. Definitely not on how much you can spend. Not even on how badly they want to make a representative team.

It depends on one simple question. **What are you trying to achieve?** Because the right amount of training for a child who just wants to have fun is completely different from the right amount for a player chasing representative basketball.

The mistake many families make is assuming there’s one magic number. There isn’t. But there are some principles that every parent should understand.

# **The biggest mistake parents make**

Let’s start with the biggest misconception. Many parents believe that games make players better. They don’t. Games reveal what players can already do. Training is where players improve.

Imagine your child wants to learn piano. Would one recital every Saturday make them a better pianist? Of course not. The improvement happens during practice.

Basketball works exactly the same way. Games are important because they teach decision making, teamwork and competition. But games don’t usually provide enough repetitions for real skill development.

A child might only take four or five shots in an entire game. They might dribble the ball for only a few minutes. They might never get to practise using their weak hand. Compare that with a one-hour training session where they might shoot over one hundred times, finish dozens of layups and handle the ball almost constantly. That’s where improvement happens.

# **More games don’t always mean more improvement**

This surprises many families. Some children play three games every weekend but only train once. Others train three times every week and play one game. Guess which child usually improves faster? The one who trains. Not because games aren’t valuable. Because repetition builds skill.

Think about the best shooters in the world. They didn’t become great because they played more games than everyone else. They became great because they repeated the same movements thousands of times until they became automatic. Children aren’t any different.

# **Confidence follows repetition**

We already talked about why children become[ scared to shoot](https://proball.com/blog/why-is-my-child-scared-to-shoot-the-real-reason-kids-stop-taking-open-shots.html). One of the biggest reasons is they don’t trust their own ability. Where does trust come from? Repetition. 

Every successful pass. Every made layup. Every good decision. Every training session adds another brick to the wall. Eventually that wall becomes confidence. That’s why the children who train consistently often look calmer during games. They’re not calmer because they’re naturally confident. They’re calmer because they’ve experienced those situations hundreds of times before.

# **So… how often should they train?**

There isn’t one answer. Here’s what we’ve found works well for most young players.

## **Ages 5 to 7**

**One to two training sessions each week.**

At this age, the goal isn’t creating elite athletes. It’s helping children fall in love with basketball. Sessions should be energetic. Fun should come first. Children who enjoy coming back every week are much more likely to keep playing for years.

## **Ages 8 to 10**

**Two training sessions each week is a great foundation.**

Children start developing stronger habits. Ball handling improves. Footwork becomes more coordinated. Confidence begins to grow. This is also the age where consistent attendance often matters more than adding extra sessions. Two quality sessions every week beat four sessions followed by two weeks away. Consistency wins.

## **Ages 11 to 13**

For many players, **two to three sessions each week** becomes the sweet spot.

They’re learning more complex skills. The game speeds up. Decision making becomes more important. This is often the age where children start thinking about representative basketball. Training regularly gives them a much stronger chance of being ready when those opportunities come.

## **Representative players**

Representative basketball usually requires another level of commitment. Most rep athletes train multiple times each week alongside games. The goal isn’t simply training more. It’s training with purpose.

Every session should have a reason. Improve shooting. Improve defence. Improve decision making. Improve conditioning. More isn’t always better. Better is better.

# **Can children train too much?**

Absolutely. More basketball isn’t automatically better basketball. Children still need time to recover. They need sleep. They need school. They need friends. Sometimes they need an afternoon where they simply kick a football around the park or ride their bike.

Burnout doesn’t happen because children love basketball. It happens when basketball stops being enjoyable. If your child starts dreading training, constantly complains about being tired or loses enthusiasm for the game, it might be time to reduce the workload for a while. Long-term development always beats short-term intensity.

# **Quality always beats quantity**

Parents sometimes ask, _"should we train every day?”_ Not if every session is the same. A child who completes two focused sessions with great energy will usually improve more than someone who drags themselves through six tired sessions.

Good training has purpose. Some days should focus on skills. Some on shooting. Some on decision making. Some should simply be about enjoying the game. Children improve fastest when they stay excited about coming back tomorrow.

# **The week we recommend**

For most children who want to improve while still enjoying basketball, a balanced week often looks like this.

- Two basketball training sessions.
- One game.
- Plenty of sleep.
- Active play outside basketball.
- One or two complete rest days.

That balance allows children to improve steadily without feeling overwhelmed. If they’re chasing representative basketball, adding another quality training session can make sense. The key word is **quality**. Not just more.

# **Final thoughts**

If you’re asking how often your child should train, you’re already asking the right question. Don’t compare your child to someone else’s schedule. Compare your child to where they were three months ago.

Are they enjoying basketball more? Are they becoming more confident? Are they trying new things during games? Are they smiling when they leave training? Those are the signs that matter. Because basketball isn’t a race. It’s a JOURNEY.
