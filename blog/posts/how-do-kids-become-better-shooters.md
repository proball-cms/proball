Every basketball parent has said it. _“We need to work on your shooting.”_ Maybe after a tough game, or after seeing a few missed layups. Perhaps after watching another child make three shots in a row.

It’s an understandable reaction. After all, basketball is about putting the ball in the basket.

Regardless, there's something else that surprises many parents. The best shooters don’t simply shoot more. They practise differently. There's an important difference here. Because if taking hundreds of random shots created great shooters, every child who spent an afternoon throwing the ball at the hoop would become an elite scorer. That doesn’t happen. The players who become great shooters usually train with purpose. And it starts long before the ball even leaves their hands.

# **Shooting starts before the shot**

Most people think shooting begins when the ball reaches your hands. It actually starts much earlier. Great shooters arrive ready. Their feet are balanced. Their hands are prepared. Their eyes are already on the basket. Their decision has already been made. That’s why great shooters often look effortless. They’re not reacting late. They’re prepared early.

Young players often do the opposite. They catch the ball. They stop. They think. They decide. By then, the defender has arrived. The shot disappears.

Good shooting isn’t just about mechanics. It’s about PREPARATION. Can't stress this enough. The best ones don't think on the court, they don't need to. They already did the hard part in practice.

# **Confidence changes everything**

We’ve coached children with beautiful shooting technique who rarely shoot in games. We’ve also coached players with unusual shooting styles who score consistently. The difference wasn’t always technique. It was confidence.

Many children already have the ability to make shots. What they don’t have is the belief that they should take them.

If your child regularly passes up open shots, the problem may not be their shooting at all. It could be their confidence. Understanding why children become afraid to shoot is often the first step towards helping them become better shooters.

# **Great shooters don’t chase perfection**

Watch Stephen Curry warm up. He misses. A lot. Watch every great shooter. They all miss. The difference is they don’t react to every miss.

Young players often do. One miss becomes, _“I’m having a bad day.”_ Two misses become,

_“I’m not a good shooter.”_ Three misses and they stop shooting altogether. That’s why emotional control matters just as much as shooting mechanics. The next shot should never depend on the previous one.

# **Repetition beats motivation**

Parents often ask, _"should my child shoot 500 shots every day?”_ Not necessarily. Five hundred poor repetitions simply teach poor habits. Twenty focused repetitions with excellent technique can be far more valuable. 

Quality always wins. The best practice sessions have intention. Players know exactly what they’re working on. One day might focus on finishing around the rim. Another might focus on catch-and-shoot situations. Another on shooting after dribbling. Improvement comes from solving one problem at a time.

# **The biggest mistake parents make**

Many parents unknowingly turn shooting into a test. Every miss becomes feedback. Every make becomes relief. Children begin measuring themselves by whether the ball goes in.

Basketball doesn’t work that way. Sometimes you take a perfect shot and miss. Sometimes you throw up a terrible shot and it somehow drops.

Instead of asking, _“did you make it?”_ Try asking, _"did that feel like a good shot?”_ That’s the question great shooters ask themselves. They judge the decision before they judge the result.

# **Games don’t create shooters**

Games reveal your shooting. Training develops it. A child might only take four or five shots during an entire game. That’s not enough repetition to improve significantly. Improvement happens during training. It happens when players can repeat the same movement again and again while receiving feedback. It happens when they’re allowed to make mistakes without worrying about the scoreboard.

# **Better shooters don’t just shoot**

This surprises many parents. Better footwork creates better shooting. Better balance creates better shooting. Better passing creates better shooting. Better decision making creates better shooting. Everything is connected. That’s why simply standing under the basket for an hour isn’t enough.

Players need to move. Catch on the run. Shoot after changing direction. Shoot while tired. Shoot after making decisions. That’s what happens in games. So that’s what training should look like.

# **What children should practise most**

If your child wants to become a better shooter, encourage them to focus on these habits.

- Balance before speed.
- Eyes on the rim.
- Finish every shot the same way.
- Practise at game speed.
- Don’t rush because of a few misses.
- Celebrate good decisions, not just made baskets.

Small improvements repeated every week become huge improvements over time.

# **Final thoughts**

Every great shooter started exactly where your child is today. Missing shots. Learning footwork. Building confidence. Trying again.

The children who become great shooters aren’t always the most talented. They’re usually the ones who stay patient. Who keep practising. Who stop chasing perfection. Who fall in love with getting a little better every week.

Help your child enjoy that process. The points will eventually take care of themselves.
