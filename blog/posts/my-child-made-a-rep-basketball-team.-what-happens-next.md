Your child made a representative basketball team. Congratulations! After weeks of trials, nervous car rides, waiting for emails and probably a few conversations about how they performed, you’ve finally received the news everyone was hoping for.

Your child is excited, you’re proud, and there’s a good chance you’ve already told the grandparents. Maybe you’ve even started looking at the competition calendar and wondering how many weekends you’re about to spend inside basketball stadiums. Once the excitement settles, though, parents usually start asking questions. What happens now?How much training will there be? Will my child get much court time? Should they keep playing domestic basketball? And what should we be doing between now and the start of the representative season?

If this is your family’s first experience with representative basketball in Sydney, there’s quite a bit to learn. You don’t need to have everything figured out immediately.

## **First, Enjoy the Achievement**

Before worrying about training schedules, team expectations or the upcoming season, give your child an opportunity to enjoy being selected. They’ve worked towards something important, competed against other talented young players and earned a place in a representative program. That’s worth celebrating.

For many children, making their first representative team is one of the biggest moments they’ve experienced in basketball. They might have spent months talking about trials, practising at home or imagining what it would feel like to wear their association’s uniform. Let them enjoy that feeling without immediately turning the conversation towards what they need to improve.

At the same time, be careful about making selection sound like they’ve finally arrived. At U12 or U14 level, children still have years of development ahead of them. Some players will improve quickly, others will take longer, and the players who look strongest today won’t necessarily be the strongest in a few years. Making a representative team is a great achievement, and there’s plenty more basketball ahead of them.

## **What Actually Changes When Your Child Starts Playing Rep Basketball?**

For most families, the biggest change is the level of commitment. If you’re new to rep, understanding [**what representative basketball involves**](https://proball.com/blog/the-ultimate-parent%E2%80%99s-guide-to-representative-rep-basketball-trials-in-sydney.html) can help you prepare for the additional team training, weekend competition, travel and sometimes tournaments or other association activities.

Your child will also be playing against stronger competition. Players are generally quicker, more experienced and more comfortable with physical contact. Decisions need to happen faster, defensive mistakes are more likely to be punished, and scoring opportunities can become harder to find.

A child who regularly scores 15 or 20 points in domestic basketball might suddenly find themselves scoring only a few points in representative games. That’s normal. They’re competing against players who were often among the strongest performers in their own domestic competitions. It can take time to adjust, particularly for children who are used to being one of the best players on their team.

## **Your Child Might Have a Different Role Than They’re Used To**

This catches plenty of families by surprise. In domestic basketball, your child might be the main ball handler, the leading scorer or the player who takes most of the important shots. They’re used to having the ball in their hands and making decisions for the team. Then they arrive at representative training and discover that several other players have been doing exactly the same thing for their domestic teams.

Suddenly, your child might be asked to play a different position, defend a particular opponent, move without the ball or contribute in ways they haven’t experienced before. It takes some getting used to, especially when they’ve spent years playing a particular role.

A player who has always been the leading scorer might need to become a better passer. A player who dominates because they’re taller than everyone else might need to improve their footwork and positioning. A guard who has always controlled the ball might need to learn how to create scoring opportunities without dribbling.

These changes can be uncomfortable at first, especially when children associate being a good basketball player with scoring points. Parents can help by recognising the smaller contributions that don’t always appear on a scoresheet. Good defensive positioning, smart passing, communication, rebounding and making the right decisions all matter.

## **Will My Child Get Equal Court Time in Rep Basketball?**

This is one of the biggest questions parents have, and it’s worth understanding before the season begins. Representative basketball is generally more competitive than domestic basketball. Depending on the association, age group and team policies, coaches may have greater discretion over substitutions and playing time. Your child might not receive the same number of minutes as every other player.

Some coaches rotate players relatively evenly, particularly at younger ages. Others make decisions based on matchups, defensive assignments, player development, attendance, effort at training or what the team needs during a particular game. Parents should familiarise themselves with their association’s playing-time policies because expectations can vary.

Court time can also change throughout the season. A child might play limited minutes in one game and have a much larger role the following week. They might struggle against a particular opponent or gradually earn more responsibility as they become comfortable with the team’s system.

If your child isn’t getting much court time, don’t immediately assume the coach has something against them. Give them an opportunity to settle into the team, encourage them to keep improving and, if concerns continue, follow the association’s communication process to ask about their development. Ask what your child can work on and what the coach would like to see from them.

Young players still need meaningful opportunities to compete. If limited playing time becomes a consistent issue, it’s reasonable to discuss how your child’s development is being supported. Those conversations are usually more productive when everyone is focused on helping the player improve.

## **Should My Child Stop Playing Domestic Basketball?**

Not necessarily. Domestic basketball can still provide valuable opportunities for representative players, particularly when they’re learning new skills or adjusting to a different role within their rep team.

Imagine your child is playing limited minutes in representative basketball but continues playing regularly in domestic competition. Those domestic games might give them opportunities to handle the ball, make decisions, practise attacking the basket and develop confidence in situations they don’t experience as often at representative level. There’s value in having that extra game experience.

Of course, the right balance depends on your child’s schedule, energy levels, age and the requirements of their representative program. Some associations have rules about domestic participation, and families should check those before committing to additional competitions.

The main consideration is whether your child has enough opportunities to develop without becoming overloaded. Playing more basketball can be beneficial when there’s enough time for recovery, school, family life and other interests.

## **How Much Extra Training Should a Rep Basketball Player Do?**

This is where some families get carried away. Your child makes a representative team, and suddenly everyone wants to increase their training. Another skills session, another shooting workout, an extra strength program and maybe some private coaching on top. Before long, a child who was enjoying basketball three times a week is training or competing almost every day, with very little time to recover.

More training isn’t automatically better. We’ve previously explained [**how much kids should train before rep trials**,](https://proball.com/blog/how-much-should-kids-train-before-rep-basketball-trials.html) and many of the same principles apply once they’ve been selected. The right amount depends on their age, physical development and existing commitments. The right amount depends on the child’s age, physical development, existing commitments, training intensity and how they’re responding to the workload.

Representative team training will usually focus on learning systems, building team chemistry, defensive responsibilities and preparing for competition. Individual skill development still matters, but additional sessions should complement the representative program rather than leave the child constantly exhausted.

At ProBall, we encourage young athletes to develop their individual skills consistently, but we also understand that children have school, family commitments and other activities. A useful question for parents is whether their child is still arriving at training with energy and enthusiasm. If they’re regularly exhausted, complaining about persistent pain or losing interest in basketball, it might be time to review their schedule.

Children need time to recover, particularly as their training and competition commitments increase. Sometimes the best thing you can do for a young player is give them a day off.

## **What Should My Child Work On Before the Rep Season Starts?**

The period between selection and the beginning of the representative season can be a good opportunity to focus on individual development. There’s no need to completely change how your child trains. We’d usually recommend identifying a few specific areas rather than trying to improve everything at once.

Ball handling under defensive pressure is particularly useful because representative players generally have less time and space to make decisions. Finishing around the basket against contact, defensive footwork, passing and shooting technique are also valuable areas to develop.

Don’t overlook decision-making either. A child might have excellent ball-handling skills during individual drills and still struggle when a defender closes the space or a second opponent rotates across to help. Training should include situations where players need to recognise what’s happening and make decisions.

Small-sided games, competitive drills and opportunities to play against different opponents can help children become more comfortable with those situations. If your child has received feedback from their representative coach, use that as a starting point. Otherwise, speak with a coach who knows their game and can help identify two or three areas worth prioritising.

## **Your Child Might Struggle at First. That’s Normal.**

One of the most common challenges for newly selected representative players is adjusting to being surrounded by other talented athletes. In their previous team, they might have been one of the strongest players. They could score regularly, handle the ball confidently and recover from mistakes without worrying too much about losing their position.

Representative basketball can feel very different. They might find themselves defending stronger opponents, making more turnovers or struggling to score. They might also become hesitant because they’re worried about disappointing their coach or making mistakes in front of new teammates.

Parents sometimes interpret this as a loss of confidence or assume their child isn’t ready for the level. Often, they’re simply adjusting. Give them time to become familiar with their teammates, understand the coach’s expectations and get comfortable in their new environment.

Ask them what they’re learning rather than focusing entirely on how many points they scored or how many minutes they played. Some of the most important improvements during a first representative season won’t be obvious from the statistics, and it might take a few months before you really notice how much they’ve developed.

## **Parents, Your Role Changes Too**

When children begin representative basketball, parents often become more invested in the outcome. There’s more travel, more expense, more competition and sometimes more pressure around performance. You’re spending weekends at stadiums, watching closely and naturally wanting your child to do well.

The way parents respond can make a real difference to how children experience the season. Try to avoid analysing every possession on the drive home. Your child probably already knows they missed three layups or made a poor pass. They don’t need a detailed breakdown before they’ve even taken their shoes off.

If they want to talk about the game, listen. If they don’t, that’s fine too. Let the coaches coach, support the team and remember that your child is still learning.

And if another player receives more court time or has a better game, resist the temptation to turn it into a comparison. Every player has different strengths and develops at a different pace. Your child will have good games, difficult games and plenty of ordinary ones. That’s part of playing competitive sport, and parents don’t need to turn every performance into a major discussion.

## **What If My Child Doesn’t Enjoy Rep Basketball?**

It’s worth talking about this because some children who make representative teams end up finding the experience more difficult than they expected.

Some love the increased competition, structured training and opportunity to play against stronger opponents. Others find the additional commitments stressful or discover that they preferred the environment they were playing in before. Children change, and their interests change too.

If your child starts saying they don’t enjoy representative basketball, try to understand what’s behind it. Are they struggling with the competition? Are they worried about making mistakes? Are they exhausted? Are they having difficulty connecting with teammates?

Sometimes those problems can be addressed with time, support and better communication. Other times, a family might need to reconsider whether the current level of commitment is appropriate.

That doesn’t mean giving up on basketball. There are plenty of ways for children to continue playing, improving and enjoying the sport. A representative jersey should never become more important than the child’s relationship with basketball.

## **Making Rep Is an Achievement. What Happens Afterwards Matters Too.**

One thing we’ve noticed in junior basketball is how much attention representative selections receive. Children work towards trials for months. Parents discuss who might make the team, which division they’ll be placed in and what the selectors are looking for. Then the teams are announced, everyone celebrates, and attention quickly shifts towards the season ahead.

The months that follow are when players attend training, learn new skills, adjust to different roles and work through difficult games. That’s where much of their development will happen.

Some children who make representative teams at U12 will continue improving rapidly. Others will develop more gradually. Players who missed selection might improve enormously over the same period. Nobody knows exactly how those players will compare in three or four years.

We recently explored [**why good basketball players sometimes miss out on rep teams**](https://proball.com/blog/why-do-good-basketball-players-sometimes-miss-out-on-rep-teams.html), and the same lesson applies here. Selection at a young age tells us very little about where a player will be in three or four years.

We encourage families to keep their attention on the things children can control. Their preparation, attitude towards learning, willingness to practise and how they respond when basketball becomes difficult. Those habits will remain valuable regardless of which team they’re playing for.

## **Enjoy the Season. They’re Still Kids.**

If your child has just made their first representative basketball team, congratulations again. It’s a fantastic opportunity, and we hope they have a wonderful experience.

There will be exciting wins, frustrating losses, early mornings, long drives and probably a few weekends when you wonder how basketball managed to take over your entire family calendar. You’ll meet other families, watch your child develop new friendships and hopefully see them grow as a player.

There will also be mistakes, difficult conversations and moments when things don’t go as expected. Try to enjoy all of it.

Your child doesn’t need to become the best player on the team this season. They don’t need to score the most points or play every minute. They need opportunities to learn, compete, contribute and enjoy being part of a team.

At ProBall, we’re proud of the young athletes who earn representative opportunities, and we want to see them continue developing throughout the season. We also know that their basketball journey will involve many more experiences, challenges and opportunities beyond their first representative team.

Celebrate the selection, support their development and let them enjoy playing basketball. They’ve earned that opportunity.
