Your child has just received their representative basketball selection results. You open the email, scan the list and find their name. Great news! They’ve been selected as a Development Player. But after the initial excitement, you probably have a few questions. Are they officially part of the representative team? Will they train with the other players? Do they get to play games? And does being a Development Player mean they’re one step away from making the main squad?

These are reasonable questions, particularly for families experiencing representative basketball for the first time. Across Sydney, basketball associations offer development opportunities for young players who haven’t necessarily been selected for the main competition roster. The confusing part is that these programs don’t all operate the same way. Some involve training alongside representative teams, while others have separate development squads with different expectations and commitments.

If your child has been selected as a Development Player for the 2027 season, here’s what you should understand before getting started.

## **What Is a Development Player in Rep Basketball?**

A Development Player is generally a young athlete selected to participate in a [representative basketball ](https://proball.com/blog/the-ultimate-parent%E2%80%99s-guide-to-representative-rep-basketball-trials-in-sydney.html)development program without necessarily being included in the main competition roster. The association has identified the player as someone who could benefit from additional coaching and development opportunities, although the exact meaning of the selection depends on the program.

Some associations invite Development Players to train alongside their representative squads, giving them exposure to stronger competition and experienced coaches. Others operate separate development sessions focused on improving individual skills and preparing children for future representative trials. There may also be differences between age groups within the same association, so it’s worth checking the specific arrangements for your child’s team.

Being selected as a Development Player is encouraging. Your child has been offered an opportunity to continue developing within an association’s basketball pathway, and that’s something worth recognising. Just make sure you understand what the position involves before telling your child they’ll be playing representative games every weekend.

## **Is a Development Player Actually Part of the Rep Team?**

This is probably the first thing parents should clarify because being associated with a representative squad doesn’t necessarily mean a child is registered to compete in official games. A Development Player might attend training sessions, participate in development activities and work with representative coaches without being included on the team’s playing roster.

Different associations have different arrangements, and the terminology can sometimes create confusion. A child might reasonably assume that making a representative development squad means they’ll be travelling to games with their teammates. If that’s not how the program operates, finding out several weeks into the season can be disappointing.

Before accepting the position, ask whether your child will be included in the official playing roster, whether they’re eligible to participate in competitions and exactly what activities are included. It’s a simple conversation that can save everyone a lot of confusion later.

## **Will My Child Get to Play Representative Games?**

Possibly, but Development Player selection doesn’t automatically guarantee game opportunities. Whether your child can participate in representative competition depends on the association’s program structure, the relevant competition regulations and their registration status. Some development programs focus entirely on training, while others may offer additional games, tournaments or opportunities to participate in squad activities.

There may also be circumstances where Development Players can be added to a representative playing roster, provided the association and competition rules allow it. However, parents shouldn’t assume that a Development Player will automatically be called up if someone is injured or unavailable. Those decisions depend on the particular program and its rules.

If your child is expecting to play every weekend with the representative team, ask the association to explain exactly what game opportunities are available. Children can handle being told that they’re joining a training-focused development program. It’s much harder when they spend the first month expecting something that was never part of the arrangement.

## **Will Development Players Train With the Representative Team?**

Training arrangements vary between associations, so this is another area where parents should ask questions before the season begins. Some Development Players may be invited to train alongside selected representative athletes, while others attend separate sessions designed to improve individual skills and prepare them for future competition.

Training with stronger players can be a valuable experience. Children are exposed to a faster pace, more physical defence and teammates who may already have a good understanding of representative basketball. They quickly discover that things they could do comfortably in domestic competition become more difficult when defenders are quicker, stronger and better organised.

Separate development sessions can also be useful, particularly when coaches have more time to work on individual weaknesses. A child who needs to improve their ball handling, shooting technique or defensive footwork may benefit from an environment where those skills receive more attention.

I’d want to know how often my child will train, who will be coaching them, whether they’ll train with the representative squad and what the coaches expect them to improve. Those answers will help you understand the value of the program and how it fits around your family’s existing commitments.

## **Can a Development Player Move Into the Representative Team?**

It’s possible in some programs, although parents shouldn’t assume that moving into the main squad is guaranteed or even available during the current season. Associations have their own selection policies, roster arrangements and eligibility requirements, and opportunities to move between squads may depend on available places and competition regulations.

Some development programs are designed primarily to prepare players for future representative trials. A child might spend the season working with association coaches, improving their skills and becoming more familiar with the expectations of representative basketball before trialling again the following year. Other programs may have different arrangements, so it’s worth asking how player progression is managed.

[If your child wants to make the representative team](https://proball.com/blog/how-can-my-child-prepare-for-rep-trials.html), encourage them to ask their coach what they should improve. Having two or three specific areas to work on is far more useful than spending the entire season wondering whether someone might leave the squad. Your child still has basketball to play, teammates to support and plenty of opportunities to get better.

## **Should My Child Keep Playing Domestic Basketball?**

For many Development Players, continuing domestic basketball can be particularly valuable. If their development program involves regular training without representative games, domestic competition provides opportunities to practise those skills against real defenders. Children can handle the ball, make decisions, attack the basket and experience situations that are difficult to reproduce in drills.

Think about a young player who is working on becoming more comfortable bringing the ball up the court under pressure. They might spend time developing that skill during representative development sessions, but they still need opportunities to use it during games. Domestic basketball can provide those opportunities, particularly when they’re given responsibility to handle the ball and make decisions.

Domestic competition also allows children to play with friends, enjoy basketball in a familiar environment and take on roles they might not receive in a representative squad. Of course, families should check their association’s eligibility requirements and consider the child’s overall workload. Between school, domestic games, development training and other activities, it’s surprisingly easy to fill every afternoon of the week.

More basketball can be beneficial when the training is appropriate and the child is enjoying it. If they’re constantly exhausted, struggling to manage schoolwork or beginning to dread training, it’s worth reviewing the schedule.

## **Is Being a Development Player Better Than Missing Out Completely?**

For some children, a Development Player position can be an excellent opportunity. They may receive additional coaching, train alongside stronger athletes and gain a better understanding of what representative basketball requires. The experience can help them identify weaknesses and become more comfortable competing at a higher level.

However, the quality of the experience matters more than the title attached to it. A child who receives good coaching, plays regularly in domestic competition and continues developing their individual skills can still make significant progress without being part of a representative development program.

Young basketball players develop at very different rates. Some children are physically ready for representative competition at 11, while others need more time to develop their coordination, confidence or understanding of the game. We’ve all seen the smaller player who struggles against taller opponents one season and looks completely different a year or two later.

Being selected as a Development Player is encouraging, but it doesn’t guarantee future representative selection. Equally, missing out on a development position doesn’t mean a child has reached their potential. At U12 and U14 level, there’s still a lot of growing and learning ahead.

## **What Should My Child Work On as a Development Player?**

I’d start by asking the development coach for two or three specific areas your child should focus on. Some players might need to improve their ball handling under defensive pressure, while others could benefit from working on finishing, shooting technique, defensive positioning or decision-making. The areas will depend on the individual player and what the coaches observed during trials.

Once you’ve identified those priorities, try to give your child opportunities to practise them regularly. If they’re working on their ball handling, for example, they should spend time dribbling against defenders and learning to make decisions under pressure. Dribbling through cones has its place, but eventually someone needs to try to steal the ball.

I’d also encourage children to become comfortable playing against different opponents. Some will be taller, quicker or stronger, and learning how to adjust is part of becoming a better basketball player. A child who struggles against physical defenders might need to improve their footwork and decision-making, while another might need to learn how to contribute when they’re not the main scorer.

Keep the goals manageable and give them time to improve. An 11-year-old doesn’t need a detailed development spreadsheet covering the next four years. A couple of useful goals, regular practice and a genuine enjoyment of basketball will give them plenty to work with.

## **What Should Parents Ask Before Accepting a Development Player Position?**

Before committing to a development program, I’d recommend asking the association a few practical questions. You want to understand what your child is being offered, what the expectations are and whether the experience suits their current stage of development.

**Five questions worth asking:**

1. **What training is included?** How often will my child train, who will coach them and will they train alongside the representative squad?
2. **Will my child play official games?** Does the position include competition opportunities, tournaments or development games, or is it training-only?
3. **What are the fees?** Are there program costs, registration fees, uniforms or other expenses we should know about?
4. **Can Development Players move into the representative team?** Is that possible during the season, and what rules or selection processes apply?
5. **What happens at the end of the season?** Will my child need to trial again, and will coaches provide feedback on their development?

I’d also ask about attendance expectations and whether the program can fit around domestic basketball, school and other activities. There’s nothing wrong with asking these questions. You’re making a commitment on behalf of your child, and you deserve to understand what that involves.

## **What If My Child Is Disappointed About Being a Development Player?**

This is worth discussing because children can have mixed feelings about their selection. They might be excited to receive an opportunity within the representative program but disappointed that they didn’t make the main team. Perhaps several friends were selected as representative players, or they were expecting to play games together throughout the season.

Give them some time to process that disappointment. There’s no need to immediately convince them that everything is wonderful, particularly if they had their heart set on making the playing squad. Listen to what they’re saying, explain what the development opportunity involves and help them decide whether it’s something they’d like to pursue.

I’d also be careful about turning the experience into a mission to prove the selectors wrong. Children already put enough pressure on themselves during trials, and they don’t need every training session to become another audition. Encourage them to focus on improving their skills, becoming comfortable against stronger opponents and enjoying the opportunity to learn from different coaches.

If they eventually make the representative team, fantastic. If they don’t, the experience can still help them become a better basketball player. There are plenty of opportunities for children to develop outside representative basketball, and their enjoyment of the game should remain a priority.
