Your child made a representative basketball team. Great news! After weeks of trials, nervous car rides and waiting for selections to be announced, they’ve earned a place in their association’s representative program.

But there’s a small problem. They didn’t make the team you were hoping for.

Maybe they were selected for the second team instead of the first. Maybe they made the third team, while several of their friends were selected for a higher division. Suddenly, what should have been a celebration feels a little disappointing. And sometimes, if we’re being fair, the parents are more disappointed than the children.

This happens every year in junior basketball. Across Sydney, associations are announcing their representative teams for 2027, and plenty of families are trying to understand what their child’s placement actually means. Should you be disappointed if your child makes a lower representative team? Does it mean they’re not as good as the players selected above them? Could playing in a lower team actually help their development?

## **First, Your Child Still Made a Representative Team**

It’s easy to forget this when everyone starts comparing team lists. Your child attended trials, competed against other players and was selected to represent their association. That’s an achievement worth recognising, regardless of whether they made the first, second or third team.

For many children, making any representative team is something they’ve been working towards for months. They’ve spent time training, practising at home and talking about trials with their friends. Then the selections come out, and instead of celebrating, the conversation immediately turns to why they weren’t placed in a higher team.

Imagine being 11 years old, excited because you’ve made rep basketball, only to hear adults discussing whether you should have been selected for a different team. It’s probably not the reaction you were hoping for when you opened that email.

If your child is happy with their selection, let them be happy. There’s no need to introduce disappointment they weren’t already feeling. And if they are disappointed, that’s understandable too. Give them time to process it before deciding what the result means.

## **What Does Being Selected for a Lower Rep Team Actually Mean?**

Most representative basketball associations divide players into different teams based on their assessments during trials and the needs of each squad. Depending on the association and competition, those teams might be labelled first, second or third teams, or use other names and divisions.

Generally, the first team is considered the strongest group at the time of selection. The second and third teams provide opportunities for other players to compete at representative level. Selections are based on what coaches and selectors observe during a relatively short period, including individual skills, athletic ability, decision-making, defensive effort, positional needs and how players perform alongside others.

If you’re wondering how those decisions are made, we’ve previously explained **[what rep coaches actually look for](https://proball.com/blog/how-to-stand-out-at-basketball-rep-trials-in-sydney.html)** during junior basketball trials.

No selection process can perfectly predict how a group of 11, 12 or 13-year-olds will develop over the next few years. A player who looks physically dominant at U12 might have a very different experience when other children catch up in height and strength. Another player might be technically skilled but still developing the confidence or physical ability to compete against stronger opponents.

Coaches are making decisions based on the information available to them, and those decisions deserve to be respected. Parents should also be careful about treating them as permanent rankings.

## **Does Playing in a Lower Rep Team Limit Your Child’s Development?**

It can affect the experience your child has, but a lower team placement doesn’t automatically limit their development. A lot depends on the coaching environment, competition level, training opportunities and the role your child plays within the team.

Consider two young players. One makes the first representative team but spends much of the season playing limited minutes. They’re surrounded by excellent players at training and regularly compete against strong opponents, which can be valuable.

Another makes the second team and becomes an important contributor. They handle the ball regularly, defend challenging opponents, make decisions under pressure and play meaningful minutes throughout the season.

Which player will improve more? We can’t answer that from their team placement alone.

Both experiences offer opportunities and challenges. The first player might benefit from training alongside stronger teammates and learning to contribute in a smaller role. The second might develop through greater responsibility and more opportunities to make decisions during games.

I’d be paying attention to the quality of coaching, the level of competition and whether your child has enough opportunities to practise what they’re learning. Those things will tell you far more about their development as the season progresses.

## **Sometimes Being a Bigger Contributor Can Help**

There’s something valuable about being given responsibility on a basketball team. When children have regular opportunities to handle the ball, make decisions and contribute during important moments, they gain experience that can be difficult to reproduce in training.

A player who spends most of their time standing in the corner during games might become very good at understanding that particular role. They may also have fewer opportunities to develop other parts of their game. In a lower representative team, that same player might be asked to bring the ball up the court, attack defenders, create opportunities for teammates or take important shots.

They’ll probably make mistakes. Plenty of them. That’s part of learning.

Of course, this doesn’t mean every child should aim to play in a lower team. Stronger competition can expose weaknesses and encourage players to improve. Training alongside talented teammates can also raise standards.

Team placement tells us very little about the quality of the opportunities a child will receive over an entire season. Parents should look at the experience their child is actually having.

## **What If Their Friends Made the First Team?**

This is often where things become difficult. At U12 and U14 level, basketball is a big part of children’s social lives. They train together, play domestic basketball together and spend weekends at the same stadiums. Naturally, many hope they’ll make the same representative team as their friends.

When one child makes the first team and another makes the second or third, it can feel personal. Your child might wonder why their friend was selected above them. They might start comparing their scoring, height, skills or performances during trials.

Parents sometimes do the same thing. We’ve all heard conversations about how one child scored more points than another during domestic games, or how someone couldn’t understand why a particular player made the first team.

Those comparisons rarely tell the whole story. Coaches may be looking for different skills, positions or combinations of players. And even if you genuinely disagree with the selections, repeatedly discussing them in front of your child probably won’t help.

If they’re upset about being separated from their friends, acknowledge that. It’s a perfectly reasonable thing for a young person to feel disappointed about. Encourage them to maintain those friendships while giving their new teammates a chance.

Some of the best friendships children develop through basketball begin when they’re placed on a team where they hardly know anyone.

## **Can My Child Move From the Second Team to the First Team?**

Yes, it’s possible, although the process depends on the association and its selection policies. Some associations reassess players each season, while others have particular rules around squad movements, development opportunities and team placements. Families should check directly with their association rather than assuming players can move between teams during the season.

A child who makes the second team at U12 might make the first team at U14. Another might remain in the second team for several seasons before improving significantly. Some players move in the opposite direction as the competition becomes stronger.

Junior basketball development is rarely predictable. Children grow at different rates, develop different skills and respond differently to coaching and competition. The player who dominates at 11 might struggle at 14. The smaller player who barely gets noticed at trials might suddenly become one of the strongest players in their age group a few years later.

We’ve seen enough junior basketball to know that early rankings can change considerably.

If your child wants to make a higher team next season, that’s a reasonable goal. Help them identify what they need to improve and give them opportunities to work on those areas. Just don’t make the entire season about moving up.

They’ve got a team to play for right now.

## **Should Parents Contact the Association About Team Placement?**

This depends on what you’re hoping to achieve. If you want to understand your child’s selection and receive constructive feedback, that’s reasonable. Most parents want to know what their child can improve, particularly when they’ve invested time and effort into preparing for trials.

However, associations may have specific policies about selection feedback, appeals and communication with coaches. Before contacting anyone, read those policies carefully.

It’s also worth considering whether you’re asking for feedback or trying to convince someone that the selectors made a mistake. A useful question might be, “What areas should my child focus on if they want to improve their chances of making a higher team next season?”

That’s more productive than demanding to know why another child was selected ahead of yours. And if your child is happy with their team placement, consider whether the conversation is even necessary.

Sometimes parents spend weeks trying to resolve something their child stopped worrying about after the first training session.

## **How Should My Child Approach Their First Season?**

Once the disappointment has settled, encourage your child to get involved with their new team. Learn their teammates’ names. Listen to their coach. Attend training consistently and look for opportunities to contribute.

If they’re used to being one of the strongest players in domestic basketball, they might discover that representative competition presents new challenges. They may need to improve their defensive positioning, decision-making, ball handling under pressure or ability to finish against stronger opponents. They might also find themselves playing a different position or taking on responsibilities they haven’t experienced before.

Rather than constantly discussing which team they should have made, focus on how they’re progressing in the team they’re actually playing for.

Are they becoming more comfortable handling pressure? Are they making better decisions? Are they defending more effectively? Are they enjoying training and building friendships?

Those are useful things to notice. And if they have a difficult game, try not to connect every mistake back to their team placement.

A missed layup doesn’t mean they belong in the third team. A good game doesn’t automatically prove they should have made the first. Kids are allowed to have ordinary basketball games without every performance becoming evidence in a selection debate.

## **What Should Parents Avoid Doing?**

The biggest mistake is allowing your disappointment to become your child’s disappointment.

Maybe you genuinely believe they deserved a higher team. You might even be right. But your child still has an entire season ahead of them, and they’re going to spend that time with the teammates and coaches they’ve been assigned.

Constantly telling them they should have made another team can make it harder for them to enjoy the experience they’re having. It can also create unnecessary pressure. Every good performance becomes proof that the selectors were wrong, while every poor performance feels like something they need to explain.

That’s a lot to put on an 11 or 12-year-old.

Avoid comparing your child with players in higher teams, particularly in front of other families. Don’t criticise the coaching staff before you’ve given them an opportunity to work with your child.

And please don’t make your child feel embarrassed about telling people which team they’ve made. They earned their place. Let them wear the jersey proudly.

## **A Year Can Make a Huge Difference in Junior Basketball**

One of the reasons we encourage parents to keep perspective is how quickly children can develop. Think about how different some children look and play between the ages of 11 and 13.

Some grow significantly. Others become quicker, stronger or more coordinated. Some develop a better understanding of the game, while others improve their shooting or ball handling through consistent practice.

A player who struggles with physical contact at U12 might become much more comfortable a year later. Another who relies heavily on being taller than everyone else might need to develop new skills when that advantage disappears.

We should be careful about making long-term predictions based on junior representative selections. Making the first team at U12 is a fantastic achievement. Making the second or third team is also something to be proud of. Neither result tells us exactly what kind of player a child will become.

Our article about **[why good basketball players sometimes miss out on rep teams](https://proball.com/blog/why-do-good-basketball-players-sometimes-miss-out-on-rep-teams.html)** explains why selection decisions at a young age shouldn’t be treated as permanent judgments.

What happens during the following years matters enormously, including the quality of their training, the opportunities they receive and whether they continue enjoying basketball enough to keep playing.

## **The Best Team for Your Child Might Surprise You**

Parents naturally want their children to receive the best opportunities available. Sometimes that means playing in the strongest representative team. Other times, a child might benefit from a different environment where they have more responsibility, feel comfortable making mistakes and receive plenty of opportunities to compete.

There’s no universal answer. A player who thrives on strong competition might love the challenge of being one of the less experienced players in a top team. Another might develop more effectively when they’re trusted to handle the ball and contribute regularly.

What matters is how your child responds to the experience. Are they learning? Are they being challenged? Do they have opportunities to improve? Are they enjoying basketball?

Those questions will tell you more about their season than the number attached to their team.

## **Celebrate the Team They Made**

If your child has just been selected for a second or third representative team, congratulations. They’ve earned an opportunity that many young basketball players work hard to achieve.

They might be disappointed about their placement, and that’s understandable. Give them some time, listen to how they’re feeling and help them look forward to the season. There will be new teammates, new coaches, stronger competition and plenty of opportunities to improve. There will also be difficult games, mistakes and moments when things don’t go as planned. That’s basketball.

At ProBall, we encourage young players to keep developing their individual skills regardless of which representative team they’re selected for. We’ve seen how much children can improve over a relatively short period, particularly when they’re training consistently and enjoying the game.

And parents, if your child is already excited about their new team, you don’t need to remind them that they could have made a higher one.

Sometimes the best thing you can do is celebrate with them, buy the team merchandise and start working out which stadium you’re driving to next weekend. 😂

**They made rep basketball. Let them enjoy it.**
