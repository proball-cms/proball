Being a sports parent is harder than it looks. You sit on the sideline watching someone you love more than anything compete in a game you can’t control. You see the missed shot. The turnover. The questionable referee call. The kid who won’t pass them the ball. The coach who leaves them on the bench longer than you’d like.

Somehow you’re supposed to sit there calmly. It’s not easy. Although after spending a lot of time around junior basketball, we’ve noticed something. The best sports parents aren’t necessarily the ones who understand basketball the most. They’re the ones who understand **what their child needs from them.** Often, that’s very different from what their coach needs to provide.

## **Let the Coach Coach**

This sounds obvious. It’s surprisingly difficult in practice. When a child is playing, they already have a lot to process. Where’s my player? What’s the coach asking us to run? Should I shoot? Should I pass? Where do I need to be defensively?

Now add Mum or Dad yelling another set of instructions from the sideline. Suddenly the child has two coaches. Sometimes those coaches are telling them completely different things.

Your child needs you at the game. They just don’t necessarily need you coaching the game. Cheer. Encourage. Celebrate great effort. Then let the coach do the coaching.

## **2. Don’t Make the Car Ride Home Another Training Session**

The game finishes. Your child gets in the car. Before you’ve left the car park: _“why didn’t you shoot that?” “You need to use your left hand more.” “You weren’t getting back on defence.” “What happened in the third quarter?”_

Most parents aren’t trying to criticise. They’re trying to help, but your child has probably just spent an hour being coached, making decisions and dealing with the emotions of competition.

They might not need another analysis session. Sometimes the best question is simply: **“did you have fun?” o**r: **“what did you think of the game?”** 

**S**ometimes they won’t want to talk at all. That’s okay too.

## **Praise Things They Can Control**

Your child can’t completely control whether they score 20 points. They can’t control the referee. They can’t control how good the opposition is. They can’t control whether every shot goes in. They can control plenty.

Their effort. Their attitude. Their preparation. Their body language. Whether they listen. Whether they defend. Whether they support teammates. Whether they keep competing after making a mistake. Those are the things worth noticing.

Instead of: **“you played amazing because you scored 15.”** Try: **“I loved how you kept competing after those two turnovers.”** Now we’re teaching children what actually matters.

## **Let Them Struggle Sometimes**

This might be the hardest one. When your child is disappointed, your instinct is to fix it. They didn’t make the team. They lost the Grand Final. They’re not starting. They missed the game-winner.

We recently wrote about [**why kids need to learn how to lose**](https://proball.com/blog/is-basketball-good-for-my-child%E2%80%99s-confidence.html), because junior sport gives children an incredible opportunity to experience disappointment while surrounded by people who love them.

We don’t need to remove every difficult feeling. Sometimes we need to stay close while they work through it. A hug might be more valuable than an explanation. Because eventually your child discovers: **“that hurt. But I’m okay.”** That’s resilience.

## **Don’t Let Their Performance Become Your Mood**

Kids notice this quickly. They play well. Mum or Dad is buzzing. They play badly. The car ride home feels different. Even when nothing is explicitly said, children can start connecting their performance with their parent’s approval. That’s a dangerous place to go.

Your relationship with your child shouldn’t change depending on whether the basketball went in. This connects closely to **how basketball can build a child’s confidence**. We don’t want confidence that says: _“I’m valuable when I play well.”_ We want: **“I’m supported whether I play brilliantly or terribly.”** That’s a much stronger foundation.

## **Don’t Compare Their Journey**

This kid made reps. That kid is playing Division 1. Someone else scored 25. Another child is already training with an older age group. It’s incredibly tempting to compare, but junior athletes develop at wildly different speeds.

Physical development matters. Confidence matters. Experience matters. Opportunity matters. Some children develop early. Others arrive later.

We’ve talked about this throughout our **representative basketball guides**, because missing a rep team at 11 doesn’t tell you what kind of basketball player a child will become at 16. Let your child’s journey belong to them.

## **Help Them Love the Game**

This one might matter more than everything else. If your child is 8, 10 or 12 years old, there’s still an enormous amount of basketball ahead of them. The fastest way to stop that journey? Make every game feel like an exam. Every missed shot becomes a problem. Every loss becomes a crisis. Every selection becomes a judgement. Every training becomes something they _have_ to do...

Yes, improvement requires work. We absolutely believe young athletes should learn discipline, consistency and how to do difficult things. Although they should also laugh. Make friends. Celebrate ridiculous shots. Talk rubbish with teammates. Love Saturday mornings. Ask to go outside and shoot a basketball because **they want to**. 

Don’t underestimate that. A kid who still loves basketball at 15 has a much better chance of discovering how good they can become than a talented 12-year-old who’s already sick of it.

## **Be Their Safe Place**

Your child’s coach has a job. Challenge them. Correct them. Push them. Hold them accountable. Teach them basketball. Parents have a different job. You can be the person they come back to when everything doesn’t go perfectly. That doesn’t mean telling them they’re always right. It doesn’t mean blaming coaches, teammates or referees.

It doesn’t mean protecting them from every consequence. It means making sure they know: **One bad basketball game doesn’t define them.** One missed shot doesn’t change how you see them. One unsuccessful trial doesn’t make them a failure. One difficult season doesn’t determine their future.

They can fail. Learn. Come home, and go again tomorrow.

## **Your Child Probably Won’t Remember the Score**

Years from now, your child probably won’t remember the score of that random Saturday morning game. They won’t remember whether they scored 8 points or 12. They probably won’t remember half the games you’re currently stressing about, but they’ll remember how basketball felt.

They’ll remember teammates. Road trips. Coaches. Big wins. Painful losses. The first time they made a team. The first time they didn’t, and they’ll remember looking into the crowd and seeing you there.

So cheer. Support them. Let them struggle. Let them grow, and every now and then, remember to enjoy watching them play. One day, there won’t be another game next Saturday, and you’ll probably miss it more than you think.
