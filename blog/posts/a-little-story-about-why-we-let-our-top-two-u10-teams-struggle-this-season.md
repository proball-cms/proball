Winning feels good. Kids love it. Parents love it. Coaches certainly aren’t complaining when the scoreboard is going their way. But when we’re talking about junior basketball, there’s a question we think matters more: **Is your child actually developing?**

Those two things aren’t always the same. Sometimes the environment that’s best for a young basketball player isn’t the one where they dominate every Saturday. Sometimes it’s the one that makes them uncomfortable. That’s exactly what we wanted for two of our youngest ProBall teams this season.

## **Our U10s Are Playing Against U12s**

Our ProBall Blazers and Hornets are U10 teams. This season, however, we decided to have them play up in the U12 competition. We knew what that meant. They’d play against kids who were older. Bigger. Stronger. More experienced.

There would be games where things happened faster than they were used to. There would be physical contact they hadn’t experienced before. There would be situations they didn’t immediately know how to solve. 

**That’s why we did it.** Not because we expected them to dominate. Because we wanted them to adapt.

## **Development Needs the Right Amount of Difficulty**

Imagine a young player who is always the fastest kid on the court. What happens when they finally meet someone faster?

Imagine a player who can simply dribble around every defender because they’re physically bigger than everyone else. What happens when that advantage disappears?

This is one of the challenges in junior sport. Success can sometimes hide weaknesses. Put that same player into a slightly harder environment and suddenly they have to find another solution. They have to pass earlier. Protect the basketball. Change direction. Communicate. Defend someone stronger. Make decisions faster. Keep competing after something doesn’t go their way. That’s development.

## **And Yes, Sometimes They’re Completely Lost** 

We aren’t going to pretend it’s been some perfect inspirational movie. They’re kids. Sometimes they still cry. Sometimes they get hit and become frustrated. Sometimes they make a mistake and look around like they have absolutely no idea what just happened. Sometimes the other team is simply bigger and better.

Then something important happens. **They go back out there.** Week after week. They compete again. And slowly, situations that once felt overwhelming begin to feel normal.

That’s one of the things we love most about putting young athletes into appropriately challenging environments. You don’t need to remove every difficult situation. You help them become capable of handling more difficult situations.

## **Confidence Isn’t Built by Making Everything Easy**

There’s a version of confidence that comes from everything going your way. Then there’s another version. You’ve played against someone bigger before. You’ve been knocked down before. You’ve turned the ball over and recovered. You’ve been losing and kept playing. You’ve been nervous and still stepped onto the court.

That creates evidence. The next time something difficult happens, the player has somewhere to look mentally: **I’ve handled this before.** For young athletes, that’s powerful.

## **Our Parents Bought Into It Too**

This part matters. Playing kids up an age group only works when everyone understands **why** you’re doing it. If the only measurement of success is Saturday’s scoreboard, the season can quickly become frustrating.

Fortunately, our Blazers and Hornets families understood what we were trying to achieve. The objective wasn’t: **Let’s see how many games our U10s can win against U12s.** It was: **Let’s see how much our U10s can grow by competing against U12s.** That changes how you experience the season.

A difficult game isn’t automatically a failure. A loss can still contain enormous progress. A player successfully handling pressure they couldn’t handle two months earlier matters. A kid getting knocked over and immediately getting back into defence matters.

Making a better decision matters. Competing matters.

## **Then Something Funny Happened**

Despite all of that… **Both teams made the U12 semi-finals.** The Blazers made it. The Hornets made it. Two teams of U10 kids who’ve spent the season competing against older players now get the chance to play finals basketball against them.

Of course we want them to win. We’re still competitors. When the ball goes up, we’re trying to beat you. But here’s the important part: **Making the semi-finals isn’t what makes this experiment successful.** Their development does. The finals are a bonus.

## **Junior Basketball Needs a Longer Time Horizon**

It’s incredibly easy in youth sport to become obsessed with what’s happening right now. Did they win? How many points did my child score? What division are they playing? Did they make the better team?

Those things can matter. But they’re snapshots. Development takes years. Believe us, we say day in and day out. Every year, the same. 

A child who’s challenged appropriately at 9 or 10 may struggle more today while building skills that become incredibly valuable at 12, 14 or 16.

That’s why coaches and parents sometimes have to resist the temptation to optimise everything around immediate success.

**We’re developing basketball players, not collecting U10 trophies.** There’s a difference.

## **The Goal Isn’t to Make Kids Miserable Either**

There’s an important balance here. Development doesn’t mean throwing children into situations they’re completely unprepared for and calling it character building.

The challenge needs to be appropriate. Too easy and there’s little reason to adapt. Far too difficult and confidence can disappear.

The sweet spot sits somewhere between the two. Hard enough that mistakes happen. Hard enough that players occasionally become uncomfortable. But achievable enough that they can adapt, experience success and recognise their own improvement. That’s where some of the best learning happens.

## **Winning Still Matters**

We don’t think winning is bad. Far from it. Learning how to compete is part of sport. Trying desperately to win a close game can teach young athletes things a training session simply can’t reproduce.

The problem starts when winning becomes **the only measurement of whether junior sport is working**. Sometimes development leads to winning. Sometimes development requires losing for a while.

And sometimes you put a bunch of U10s into an U12 competition because you think it’ll make them better…and the little maniacs make the semi-finals anyway.

## **Blazers and Hornets, This One’s for You**

Before they take the court this week, we wanted to give these young hoopers some love. You’ve competed against bigger kids. You’ve competed against older kids. You’ve been frustrated. You’ve made mistakes. You’ve probably confused your coaches once or twice. 

Man, these kids are relentless. That’s what we’re proud of.  Whatever happens in the semi-finals doesn’t change what you’ve already accomplished this season.

**Blazers. Hornets. Go compete.**

We’ll worry about the scoreboard afterwards.
